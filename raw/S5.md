Multinational retailers that employ the fast fashion strategy include Shein,[4] Temu,[5] H&M,[6] ASOS,[7] Peacocks,[8] Fashion Nova,[9] Forever 21,[10] Uniqlo,[6] Edikted,[11] Zara[12]and C&A.[13]

Fast fashion brands have faced legal and regulatory issues due to alleged exploitation of labour, use of dangerous goods in their products, greenwashing their environmental impacts, and violations of intellectual property laws.

In the 2010s and 2020s, fast fashion trends spread quicker via social media services such as Instagram, Pinterest, and TikTok.[25][26] Through the creation of promotional opportunities, such as haul videos from influencers, fast fashion companies have been able to advertise and market their clothing towards Generation-Z.[27] As haul videos provide an emotional trigger that stimulates impulse buying, fast fashion brands and Shein in particular, are able to motivate their customers to engage in purchasing.[28] This impulse purchasing is defined as overconsumption, which has led to an increase in the amount of clothing produced and allowed for clothing to be easily disposed of after a few wears. The increase in speed of production and trend cycles has been defined as ultra-fast fashion.[29]. Many of the companies with a high social media presence, such as Shein, Fashion Nova, and PrettyLittleThing, promote ultra-fast fashion.[30]

Environmental pressures occur throughout the textile supply chain, from fibre production and dyeing to manufacturing, transportation, use and disposal. A scientific review published in 2020 estimated that the fashion industry produces more than 92 million tonnes of waste and consumes approximately 79 trillion liters of water annually. The review identified water consumption, chemical pollution, carbon emissions and textile waste as major environmental impacts associated with the industry.[36] Furthermore, the fashion industry is estimated to contribute approximately 2-8% of global greenhouse gas emissions.[37] A 2024 study comparing fast-fashion and traditional jeans consumption estimated that the carbon footprint per wear of fast-fashion jeans was 11 times higher than that of traditional fashion consumption.[38] A 2026 life cycle assessment of fast-fashion polyester and cotton T-shirts found that polyester garments generally had higher environmental impacts than cotton garments, particularly in terms of greenhouse gas emissions. The study estimated emissions of 9.62 kg of carbon dioxide equivalent for polyester T-shirts and 6.10 kg for cotton T-shirts under the conditions examined. It also found that extending the lifespan of garments could reduce their carbon emissions by approximately 50%, highlighting the environmental effects of short clothing lifespans.[39]

However, specific fast fashion brands, such as Shein, argue that their business reduces the effect of clothing waste. Their brand focuses on current trends, highlighting items that customers are inclined to buy. By sticking to the desired, they end up with less excess items unsold. Therefore, their clothing waste is minimal compared to other stores. This extends as customers rewear their Shein items multiple times, and when time to discard their clothes, the data demonstrates consumers typically gift their old pieces.[44]

Many fast fashion brands have also faced legal and regulatory issues because they produce clothing using non-biodegradable petroleum-based synthetic fibers and microplastics as well as hazardous chemicals including PFAS, endocrine disruptors, and carcinogens. They have been accused of greenwashing by hiding or misrepresenting their true impact on carbon emissions and plastic pollution. Shein was fined €1million in Italy for misleading environmental claims about its products.[49] In response, some brands, such as H&M, have banned the use of PFAS, purchased carbon offsets and credits and promoted textile recycling.

In 2013, Bangladesh was one of the top countries for clothing exports. Rana Plaza, an eight story building in Bangladesh, was used for fast fashion manufacturing. On April 23, 2013, the structure was evacuated due to the discovery of building cracks which compromised safety. However, over 3,600 employees were forced to return to the Plaza the next day. When the power went out, the building shook and collapsed, killing more than 1,000 workers of all ages and leaving over 2,500 wounded.[64] For many of the injured, untrained individuals amputated trapped workers without anesthesia or proper medical equipment.[65]
