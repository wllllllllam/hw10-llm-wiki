## 原文摘錄（英文原句，只留和本 Wiki 有關的段落）

In 2020, it was found that voluntary, self-directed reform of textile manufacturing supply chains by large companies to reduce the environmental impacts was largely unsuccessful.[4][5] Measures to reform fashion production beyond greenwashing require policies for the creation and enforcement of standardized certificates, along with related import controls, subsidies,[6] and interventions such as eco-tariffs.[7][8][9]

Slow fashion is a proposed sustainable alternative to fast fashion.[1] The term was coined by Kate Fletcher of the Centre for Sustainable Fashion and inspired by "slow food".[50] It intends to challenge fast fashion's focus on mass-production and globalized style.[51]

The concept of slow fashion has been criticized. To stop consuming "fast fashion" strikes against low-income consumers whose only means to access trends is through cheap and accessible goods.[54] Those who are already in a high position in society can afford to slow down and cement their status and position, while those on their way up resent being told to stay at the lower rungs of the status hierarchy.[55][56]

Secondhand shopping, referring to the act of buying clothing or goods that have been previously owned by someone else, is also an alternative to fast fashion.[57][58] In recent years, resale platforms such as Depop, The RealReal, and Vestiaire Collective have accelerated consumer interest in circular fashion by promoting the resale and reuse of clothing among Gen Z shoppers.[59] While this growing popularity encourages sustainable practices, it also can take away from the people who truly need affordable shopping options.[60]

In a circular economy, all forms of waste are returned to the economy or reused efficiently, i.e., the value of the materials is maintained as long as possible. In a circular fashion economy, products are consumed as long as their maximum value is retained. Products are designed for durability, and once discarded, they become raw material for new manufacturing, closing the loop through methods like rental, repair, recycling, and second-hand sales.[49]

Fashion rental and clothing swapping are models that are also known as collaborative fashion consumption; their environmental impact and mitigation of pollution are debated.[102][103] Transportation between users and storage, dry-cleaning, and repackaging causes more environmental impact than reselling or hand-me-downs.[104][105] As noted by Levänen et al. (2021), the lowest global warming impacts are achieved by reducing consumption, followed by reusing and recycling.[103] Tailored couture is another approach, with the idea being that tailored clothing can reduce mass-production.[106] Open-source content is a concept that builds on the sharing of patterns of clothing.[107] Examples of open-source fashion range from exchanging production techniques to materials, patterns and makerspaces.[108]

The most sustainable fibers in fashion are the ones many people already have. Thus, to recirculate existing garments, new business models engage the resale, revival, and recirculation of used, second-hand or vintage clothing.[109] Other resale models also contain elements of upcycling and repairs.[110]

Textile recycling firms process about 70% of the donated clothing into industrial items such as rags or cleaning cloths.[115] However, 20–25% of the second-hand clothing is sold into an international market.[115] Where possible, used jeans collected from America, for example, are sold to low-income customers in Africa for modest prices, yet most end up in landfills as the average U.S.-sized customer is several sizes bigger than the global average.[118] In a 2025 systematic review on life cycle assessments in the fashion industry, it was found that inconsistent assessment practices and lack of availability of reliable data were challenges to comparing environmental impacts of clothes.[119]

Upcycling in fashion is the process of reusing the unwanted and discarded materials into new materials or products without compromising the value and quality of the used material. The definition of textile waste can be production waste, pre-consumer waste and post-consumer waste.[120] Biomimicry suggests a perspective emphasizing the "Wisdom of Nature", where the industry looks into materials in tune with natural cycles.[121] Biomimicry replicates the cycles of nature. Materials should be biocompatible, combining biodegradable fibers with processes of fermentation and gasification.[109] Biofabrication refers to the process of using microorganisms to produce materials used by humans.[122]

The increase in western consumers' environmental interest is motivating companies to use sustainable and environmental arguments solely to increase sales. Because environmental and sustainability issues are complex, it is also easy to mislead consumers. Companies can use sustainability as a "marketing ploy", which is something that can be seen as greenwashing.[177] Greenwashing is the deceptive use of an eco-agenda in marketing strategies.[32] It refers mostly to corporations that make efforts to clean their reputation because of social pressure or for the purpose of financial gain.[178]

A major controversy on sustainable fashion concerns how the "green" imperative is used as a cover-up for systemic labor exploitation, social exclusion and environmental degradation, what is generally labelled as "greenwashing". In this, market-driven sustainability addresses sustainability to a certain degree, as brands still need to sell more products in order to be profitable. Thus, almost any initiative towards addressing ecological and social issues still contributes to the damage. In a 2017 report, the industry projects that the overall apparel consumption will rise by 63%, from 62 million tons today to 102 million tons in 2030.[179]
